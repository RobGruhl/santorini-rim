1Inside the Crater

On the tender coming in, or on the coach climbing up from Athinios

Short 0:29
Read the long version

Before anything else, take a long look at the cliff, because it's the best history book on the island and you can read it from the water. You're inside a volcano, or more exactly inside the hole where the middle of one used to be. Over a few hundred thousand years this island erupted again and again, each time throwing out ash and pumice and then sagging as the chamber underneath emptied. The bands of red, black and grey running across the cliff are those eruptions stacked one on top of another, like the pages of a book that someone has cut straight through.

The pale layer at the very top, the one the white villages are sitting on, is the most recent big chapter. About thirty-six hundred years ago, in the late Bronze Age, the island blew apart in one of the largest eruptions people have ever lived through. Ash fell across the Aegean, and when the chamber below was empty, the middle of the island fell in and the sea rushed into the gap. That collapse deepened the great bay you're crossing now, and in places the water under you is a few hundred metres deep.

The dark islands out in the middle are the volcano starting over. They've been building up from the sea floor in fits since before the Romans, and the last eruption there was in nineteen fifty. If this were a campaign, this is the moment the party works out that the whole map is the rim of a crater, with something still alive underneath it.

The people who lived here before the great eruption had a fine town on the south coast, at a place now called Akrotiri, with houses two and three storeys high, drains under the streets and painted walls. When archaeologists dug it out, starting in the nineteen sixties, they found the streets and the furniture but hardly any valuables and no bodies at all, so it seems the inhabitants felt the earthquakes, took the hint and left in time. Where they went is still an open question.

When you get up to the rim, whether by cable car, on foot, or by coach up the zigzag road from Athinios, turn round and look back down at this water. You'll be standing on that pale top layer all day.

This is where the walking starts. Firostefani means, roughly, the crown of Fira, and it sits on the rim just north of the main town, which you can see spilling down the cliff to the south. For the next couple of hours you'll keep the caldera on your left. The path follows the old track that joined the rim villages to one another, and for most of its history it was used by people moving water, wine and grain on mules rather than by anyone stopping to admire the view.

From here you can make out most of the ring. You're standing on the big crescent, the main island. The long, lower island on the far side of the water is Thirasia, which is a piece of the same rim that the sea broke through, and the little pale one further south is Aspronisi, the white island. The dark, rough heaps in the middle are Nea Kameni and Palea Kameni, which mean the new burnt island and the old one.

The island has had a lot of names, and each one tells you who was looking at it. Ancient writers remembered it as Strongyle, the round one, which is what it would have looked like before the collapse, and as Kalliste, the most beautiful. Herodotus says that a Spartan named Theras later brought settlers here and the island took his name, Thera, which is still its official name in Greek. Their city stood on a high ridge on the far side of the island to the south-east, and its ruins are still up there, looking out over the open sea.

The name Santorini came much later, from Saint Irene. It's what sailors from Venice and the rest of western Europe called the island once they began passing through in the Middle Ages, probably after a church dedicated to her near the shore. So the Greeks have called this place Thera for well over two and a half thousand years, while most of the rest of the world uses the sailors' name.

When you're ready, follow the path north. The next village along is Imerovigli, a little higher up, and it's a gentle climb on paving most of the way.

3The Lookout

Imerovigli, the highest village on the rim

Short 0:28
Read the long version

Imerovigli is the highest village on the rim, a little over three hundred metres above the water, and its name comes from vigla, the Greek word for a lookout. The usual explanation is that watchers kept an eye on the sea from up here. You could see a ship coming from a long way off, and in the centuries when this part of the Aegean was a hunting ground for pirates, that warning was worth a great deal.

It helps to picture the Aegean of the fourteen and fifteen hundreds as a contested sea full of independent operators. There were corsairs from North Africa, and there were the Knights you met in Rhodes, who raided Muslim shipping as a matter of course and weren't always fussy about whose ships they took. There were freelance captains working for whoever paid them best, and there were the fleets of Venice and the Ottoman Empire, which used all of the others when it suited them. A small island had no navy of its own. What it had was early warning, a defensible place to run to, and walls. On Santorini the walls were built into the villages themselves, and the defensible place was the rock you can see sticking out into the caldera just ahead.

Barbarossa, the most famous of the Ottoman admirals, swept through these islands in the fifteen thirties, and over the following decades Santorini ended up paying its taxes to the Sultan instead of to Venetian lords. For most people that changed who collected the money more than how they lived, and as Jamie's track explains in more detail, the islanders ran most of their own affairs for the next two and a half centuries.

These days Imerovigli is the quiet, expensive end of the rim, with hotels built into the cliff and their pools stacked like terraces below the path. If you want a coffee or a bottle of water, this is the place to get it, because after the rock the path leaves the villages behind, and there's very little shade and almost nothing to buy until Oia. If the north wind is blowing today, you'll feel it from here on.

4The Castle on the Rock

The viewpoint above Skaros Rock, just past Imerovigli

Long 2:02
Short 0:28
Read the long version

That knob of dark rock pushing out into the caldera is Skaros, and for several hundred years it held the most important town on the island. After the Fourth Crusade broke up the Byzantine Empire in twelve oh four, a Venetian adventurer named Marco Sanudo took Naxos and the islands around it and set himself up as Duke of the Archipelago. Santorini went to one of his allies as a fief, and the castle on Skaros became the seat of the island's lords.

If you're picturing a castle like the ones in Rhodes, with towers and a great gatehouse, it's worth adjusting the picture. The castles of the Cyclades were mostly built the other way round. The houses were set wall to wall around the top of the rock so that their back walls made up the outer defence, with small windows facing out and doors facing in. Inside were the lord's house, the churches, including the Catholic cathedral, and the homes of the families who mattered. At its height there were said to be a couple of hundred houses up there, on a rock that looks today as if it could barely hold a dozen. The only way in was a narrow path, and when raiders landed, people from the villages around came up and the gate was shut behind them.

That design worked very well against pirates and very badly against earthquakes. The rock is volcanic, and over time quakes and erosion cracked the buildings and sent parts of the summit sliding into the sea. Once the pirate threat faded in the seventeen hundreds, people began to move out onto the rim, where there was room to build, and by the early eighteen hundreds Skaros was more or less empty. Fira, the main town just south of where you started walking, grew partly out of that move.

On the seaward side of the rock there's a small white chapel called Theoskepasti, which means sheltered by God. If your group has time to climb Skaros, it takes most of an hour there and back, and the path is steep and loose in places. If not, this is the best place to see it anyway, with the caldera behind it and the whole broken ring around it.

5Vines in Baskets

On the open path north of Imerovigli, with the villages behind you

Long 2:12
Short 0:27
Read the long version

Out here the villages fall away, and you get to see what the island looks like when it isn't wearing its postcard face. Look across to the inland side, away from the caldera. The ground slopes gently down towards the east coast in a patchwork of low stone walls, pale volcanic soil and fields that look, at first, as though nothing much is growing in them.

Look more closely at those low, round tangles on the ground. They're vines, and they're grown that way on purpose. Santorini's farmers coil each vine into a kind of basket, called a kouloura, lying right on the soil, and they wind each year's growth into it, so that after a few decades it looks like a nest. The basket keeps the wind from stripping the shoots, and the grapes hang inside it, out of the sun and the salt spray. There's almost no rain in summer, and the old explanation is that the vines live on the moisture that comes in off the sea at night and settles into the soil, which holds it like a sponge.

The vines have another odd distinction. In the late eighteen hundreds a tiny insect called phylloxera destroyed most of the vineyards of Europe, and nearly every vine on the continent now grows on American roots grafted on to resist it. The pest never managed to settle in Santorini's volcanic sand, so the roots here are the island's own, and some of them are very old indeed, even though the baskets above them are renewed from time to time.

The grape to look for is Assyrtiko, which makes a dry white wine with plenty of acidity and a faintly salty, mineral finish that goes very well with seafood. The other local speciality is Vinsanto, a sweet wine made from grapes that are dried in the sun after picking and then aged in barrels for years, until it turns the colour of strong tea.

The island's other well known crops grow in the same dry soil. There's fava, a yellow pulse that's cooked down to a smooth purée and served with olive oil, onion and capers, and there are the small, thick-skinned Santorini tomatoes, which go into fritters that you'll find on nearly every menu. If there's time for food in Oia, a plate of fava, some tomato fritters and a glass of Assyrtiko make a very good lunch.

6The High Chapel

The little chapel of Profitis Ilias, the high point of the trail

Long 2:18
Short 0:27
Read the long version

This little chapel is dedicated to the prophet Elijah, and it marks the highest point of the trail. From here the path drops gently towards Oia, which you can see spread along the far tip of the island, white against the dark rock.

This is a good place to think about the eruption that made the view. About thirty-six hundred years ago, the people of Akrotiri, the town on the south coast you heard about at the start, felt a run of earthquakes strong enough to damage their houses. They cleared the streets, piled up the rubble, and seem to have started on repairs. Then the volcano began in earnest, and they left. The eruption buried their town under metres of pumice and ash, which is why the houses survived with their painted walls intact. Those paintings, of swallows and lilies, of two boys boxing, and of a young fisherman holding up his catch, are some of the loveliest things to survive from the Bronze Age anywhere.

In the twentieth century a Greek archaeologist named Spyridon Marinatos argued that this eruption, with the waves and ash it sent across the sea, helped bring down the Minoan palaces of Crete, about a hundred kilometres to the south. Out of that idea grew a more famous one, that Plato's story of Atlantis, the rich island kingdom that sank in a single day and night, was a distant memory of Santorini. The dates don't really fit, and most scholars think Plato invented Atlantis to make a philosophical point. Still, if you were a storyteller looking for a real place where a seafaring civilisation lost its island to fire and water, you could hardly find a better one than the ground under your feet. Marinatos went on to lead the excavation at Akrotiri, and when he died there in an accident on the dig in nineteen seventy-four, he was buried at the site, where his grave still stands by the entrance.

Before you head down, look out to sea on your right, past the north-east coast. Somewhere under that water, about seven kilometres offshore, is another volcano, called Kolumbo. In sixteen fifty it broke the surface, and the gas it gave off drifted across this end of the island and killed dozens of people and many animals. It's still active, and Greek scientists keep a close watch on it, along with the rest of this volcanic field.

7The Captains' Town

Oia, on the main lane or at the castle viewpoint at the western tip

Long 2:15
Short 0:28
Read the long version

Welcome to Oia, at the northern tip of the island. Today it's famous for its sunsets, its blue domes and the crowds that come to see both, but for most of its history it was a working port, and a remarkably prosperous one.

In the nineteenth century Oia was a town of sea captains. Its families owned and sailed merchant ships across the eastern Mediterranean and into the Black Sea, carrying grain, wine and other cargo, and the money came home and went into houses. As you walk along the main lane, look for the bigger mansions with their neoclassical fronts, tall doors and courtyards. Those were the captains' houses, built on the gentler upper slope, where there was room. The sailors and their families mostly lived in houses dug into the cliff below, which were much cheaper to make and stayed cool in summer. The village was laid out by rank, and you can still read that on the slope.

Steamships put an end to most of that fleet in the early twentieth century, and then, in July nineteen fifty-six, a large earthquake struck the sea between here and the island of Amorgos. On Santorini, dozens of people were killed and thousands of buildings were damaged, and Oia was among the worst hit. Many families left for Athens and Piraeus, and for a couple of decades much of the village stood empty and half ruined. The Greek state began restoring it in the nineteen seventies, partly as guest houses, and the village was protected, which is why even the new building here is made to look old.

At the western end of the lane are the remains of Oia's own Venetian castle, a smaller cousin of the one on Skaros, and that's where the crowds gather for the sunset. Tonight's sunset isn't until nearly seven, when you'll be back aboard, so enjoy the view in daylight. Below the castle a long flight of steps runs down to Ammoudi Bay, a tiny harbour with fish tavernas right at the water's edge. It's lovely, but it's about three hundred steps each way, so only go down if you're sure you have time to climb back.

If you have free time here, find a table in the shade and order the plate you heard about on the trail, with a glass of Assyrtiko, or a small Vinsanto afterwards if you'd like something sweet.

8Fira and the Way Down

Long 1:48

Read the long version

If your day ends in Fira, you're in the island's main town, which grew up along this stretch of the rim from the late seventeen hundreds, as people moved away from the old castle on Skaros. It's now the busiest place on the island, and on a cruise day the lanes between the cable car and the cathedral can be very full indeed.

Down below is the old port, where the island's goods came in and went out for most of its history. For a long time the only ways up were on foot or on the back of a donkey or mule, up the zigzag of about six hundred steps you can see on the cliff face. In nineteen seventy-nine the Nomikos family, local shipowners, paid for the cable car, and its takings have gone to support the island's community ever since. The donkeys still work the steps, though there are rules now about how much weight they're allowed to carry.

The cliff the cable car runs down has one more story in it. The pale volcanic ash near the top, which became known as Santorin earth, makes a very good cement when it's mixed with lime, and it sets even under water. It was dug and shipped out of here for a long time, and the island likes to say that some of it went into the building of the Suez Canal in the eighteen sixties. The scars of the old quarries are still visible on the cliffs further south.

If you have a little time before heading down, the Museum of Prehistoric Thera, a short walk from the cathedral near the bus station, holds some of the painted walls and pottery from Akrotiri, including a fresco of blue monkeys and jugs painted with swallows. It's a small museum, but it's a good way to end a day that began with that eruption.

When you go back down, whether by cable car or by coach to Athinios, look once more at the layers in the cliff on the way. You've spent the day walking along the top of them.

1Living on a Volcano

On the tender coming in, or on the coach climbing up from Athinios

Long 2:07
Short 0:30
Read the long version

As you come in, look up at the cliff and the line of white villages along its top edge, like snow along a ridge. Everyone who has ever lived on this island has lived on a volcano, and they've always known it. The ground shakes here often, the crater on the dark island in the middle still lets out wisps of steam, and that island only rose from the sea about three hundred years ago, the newest in a line of eruptions going back more than two thousand years. The last of them was in nineteen fifty.

About thirty-six hundred years ago there was a town on the south coast, at a place now called Akrotiri. It had paved streets, houses two and three storeys high, drains under the lanes, and rooms painted with swallows and lilies, with young women gathering saffron and a young man carrying fish. When the island began to shake before the great eruption, the people seem to have swept the rubble into piles and started on repairs, and then, at some point, decided to go. When archaeologists dug out the town in the nineteen sixties and seventies, they found beds, storage jars and the weights from looms, but almost nothing valuable and not a single body. People took what they could carry and left.

That choice, to watch the ground and leave when it speaks, comes up again and again in the island's history. It happened after the earthquake of nineteen fifty-six, when many families left for Athens and never really came back. And it happened again quite recently. At the start of twenty twenty-five, thousands of small earthquakes rattled the sea between here and the neighbouring island of Amorgos over a few weeks. Schools closed, a state of emergency was declared, and more than ten thousand people, residents and seasonal workers alike, left on ferries and extra flights. The tremors faded by the spring and most people came back, though the experience is still fresh for many of them.

So as you look up at those white houses on the edge, it's worth knowing that they belong to people who have made a long, practical peace with the ground they live on.

2Houses in the Rock

Firostefani, on the rim just north of Fira, where the hike begins

Long 1:56
Short 0:29
Read the long version

This is where the walk starts. As you set off north, with the caldera on your left, take a minute to look at how the houses on the cliff are put together, because they tell you a great deal about how people lived on an island with little wood and even less water.

Many of the houses here weren't so much built as dug. The cliff is made of layers of volcanic ash and pumice that are soft enough to cut with hand tools and firm enough to stand up once you have, so families carved their rooms straight back into the slope. These are called hyposkafa, which simply means dug underneath. Only the front wall had to be built, and the rooms behind it stayed cool in summer and held their warmth in winter. A typical one is long and narrow, with the living room at the front near the light, and the bedroom and the storage further back in the dark.

Where houses were built rather than dug, look at the roofs. Most are low barrel vaults or domes rather than flat roofs laid on beams, because there were almost no trees on the island, and timber had to be brought in by sea at great cost. A vault made of stone and volcanic mortar needs no beams at all.

Water shaped the house as much as the rock did. Santorini has no rivers and very few springs, so the rain that fell in winter had to last all year. Roofs and courtyards were plastered and sloped to channel the rain into a cistern under the house, and a household's cistern was one of its most precious possessions, cleaned and guarded with real care. Today most of the island's water comes from desalination plants, and many of the old cisterns sit quietly under the terraces of the hotels.

As you walk, you'll notice that you're often looking straight down onto other people's roofs and courtyards. In a village built like this, the roof of one house can be the terrace or even the path of the next, and privacy depends as much on good manners as it does on walls.

3Who Goes

Imerovigli, the highest village on the rim

Long 2:14
Short 0:30
Read the long version

Imerovigli is the highest village on the rim, and its name comes from vigla, a lookout. For centuries people kept watch from these heights for ships on the horizon, because the Aegean was full of pirates, and a little warning could mean the difference between hiding your family and losing them. Life on this island was organised around that watchfulness, and also around a question that comes up again and again in its history, which is who stays and who goes.

The oldest version of that question is told by Herodotus. Around six hundred and thirty years before Christ, he says, Thera suffered a drought that lasted seven years. The islanders asked the oracle at Delphi what to do, and were told to send settlers to Libya, on the coast of North Africa. That was a hard instruction for a small island, and the way they carried it out tells you how families were weighed against one another. From every household with two or more brothers, one brother was chosen by lot to go.

An inscription found at Cyrene, the city they founded, claims to preserve the original agreement, and its terms are remarkably practical. Anyone chosen who refused to sail could be put to death and lose his property. But if the new settlement failed after five years of honest effort, the settlers were allowed to come home and take back their land and their place as citizens, and if it succeeded, more Therans could follow later and share in it. Leaving was treated as something the whole community did together, with obligations running both ways.

Cyrene did succeed, and it became far richer and larger than Thera ever was. The island kept on doing this. In the nineteenth century its sailors settled in ports around the Black Sea and the eastern Mediterranean, and after the earthquake of nineteen fifty-six thousands of islanders moved to Athens and further abroad. Many families here still have relatives elsewhere who come home in summer for a saint's day or a wedding.

If you'd like water or a coffee, this is the last good place to get one before the open path. When you're ready, the rock sticking out into the caldera just ahead is Skaros, where the islanders once ran when the lookouts raised the alarm.

4Living Inside the Walls

The viewpoint above Skaros Rock, just past Imerovigli

Long 2:16
Short 0:30
Read the long version

The rock sticking out into the caldera is Skaros, and for several hundred years it held the island's most important town. There's very little left on top now, but if you'd come here in the fifteen hundreds you would have found a small, dense settlement of a couple of hundred houses packed onto the summit, their back walls joined into one blank outer wall with tiny windows. That was the castle. There was no separate keep or tower. The houses themselves were the defence, and everyone living inside depended on their neighbours keeping their part of the wall in good repair.

The castle was built by Venetian lords who took over these islands after the Fourth Crusade in the early twelve hundreds. They brought the Catholic church with them, and for centuries the island had two churches side by side, the Latin church of the ruling families and the Greek Orthodox church of most of the farmers and sailors. Skaros held the Catholic cathedral and the bishop's house. The two communities kept their own priests, their own feast days and, after the fifteen eighties, even their own calendars, so for long stretches they didn't celebrate Easter on the same day. A small Catholic community still lives in Fira today, with its own cathedral and a convent of nuns.

In the fifteen hundreds the islands passed to the Ottoman Empire. That sounds like a dramatic change, but in practice the Sultan's government was far away and mostly wanted taxes and quiet. Santorini, like the rest of the Cyclades, was left largely to run itself through elected local notables, who shared out and collected the taxes, settled disputes and spoke for the island to the outside world. The churches kept their property, and the bishops of both faiths had a good deal of say over everyday matters such as marriage and inheritance. When the Greek War of Independence began in eighteen twenty-one, the islanders were divided about joining, and some of the Catholic families held back. By eighteen thirty, Santorini was part of the new Greek state.

As the pirates faded and earthquakes cracked the rock, people moved out of Skaros onto the rim, and by the early eighteen hundreds it was nearly empty. On its seaward side you can see the little white chapel of Theoskepasti, whose name means sheltered by God.

5A Living from Dry Ground

On the open path north of Imerovigli, with the villages behind you

Read the long version

Out here, between the villages, you can see the island's other face, the farmland that fed it, sloping gently away from the cliff towards the east coast. For most of its history Santorini was an island of small farmers. Most families held a few scattered plots and worked them by hand, with a mule or a donkey if they were lucky, and on many of these islands the family house traditionally went to a daughter when she married, as part of her dowry.

The farming had to suit a place with no rivers and almost no rain from May to October. Look for the low, round tangles on the ground. They're vines, coiled year after year into a basket called a kouloura that lies on the soil, so the wind can't tear the shoots and the grapes hang in the shade inside. The volcanic soil holds on to whatever moisture it gets, and the islanders say the vines drink the damp that comes in off the sea at night.

The year was organised around these crops. Barley and the yellow pulse called fava were sown after the first autumn rains and harvested in early summer. The small, thick-skinned tomatoes were grown without watering, and from the late eighteen hundreds they were turned into tomato paste in factories along the coast, which gave work to many of the island's women until the last of them closed in the late twentieth century. The grape harvest came in August and was the most sociable time of the year. Families and neighbours picked together, the grapes were trodden in stone treading floors, and some were laid out in the sun to dry for the sweet Vinsanto wine.

Work came from the ground in another way too. The pale volcanic ash in the cliffs, which became known as Santorin earth, made an excellent cement, and quarrying it and loading it onto ships was steady work for generations of men, until the quarries were closed in the late twentieth century.

Tourism has changed this landscape quickly. Fields have become building plots, and many younger islanders work in hotels and restaurants rather than in the vineyards. But the vineyards on this side of the island are still worked, and plenty of families still make their own wine, much as their grandparents did.

6Saints on the Hilltops

The little chapel of Profitis Ilias, the high point of the trail

Long 2:25
Short 0:29
Read the long version

This little chapel is dedicated to the prophet Elijah, Profitis Ilias in Greek, and it marks the highest point of the trail. All over Greece, chapels to Elijah sit on hilltops like this one. The usual explanation is that he rose to heaven in a chariot of fire, so he belongs up high, and some folklorists have also noticed how close his name sounds to Helios, the old god of the sun, whose hilltop shrines he may have quietly taken over. Either way, people have been climbing to pray in places like this for a very long time.

Santorini has hundreds of small chapels, far more than its people would ever need for Sunday services. Many were built by a single family, often to keep a promise. A sailor caught in a storm might vow to build a chapel to Saint Nicholas if he reached home safely, and when he did, he built it. The chapel then belonged to that family, who looked after it, whitewashed it, lit its lamp, and opened it on its saint's day, when the priest came to say the liturgy and the family, the neighbours and anyone passing shared food together afterwards. Those feast days, called panigyria, are still a big part of the island's summer and autumn, and for families who've moved away they're often the reason to come home.

The island has older and darker beliefs as well. In the sixteen hundreds a French Jesuit priest living here, François Richard, wrote about the islanders' fear of the vrykolakas, the restless dead who were believed to rise from their graves and trouble the living. Santorini became so closely linked with them that a Greek saying, sending vampires to Santorini, meant much the same as carrying coals to Newcastle. One explanation offered later was that the volcanic soil was slow to break the dead down, so that when a grave was opened after a few years for the bones to be reburied, which is still the custom in Greece, the body was sometimes found less decayed than people expected, and that was taken as a sign that the dead person wasn't at rest. The remedy was usually a mixture of the priest's prayers and much older practices.

So when you pass a chapel on the walk, or a lamp burning in a small roadside shrine, it's worth remembering that each one stands for a particular family's promise, kept up year after year. From here the path runs gently downhill towards Oia.

7Captains and Households

Oia, on the main lane or at the castle viewpoint at the western tip

Long 2:17
Short 0:29
Read the long version

Welcome to Oia. It's one of the most visited villages in Greece, but it was built by a seafaring community, and you can still read that community in its streets.

In the nineteenth century Oia's families owned and sailed a sizeable fleet of merchant ships, trading around the eastern Mediterranean and into the Black Sea. The village was laid out by rank. The captains and shipowners built their big houses along the upper part of the village, on the gentler slope, with neoclassical fronts, high ceilings and courtyards. Ordinary sailors and their families mostly lived lower down, in houses dug into the cliff, which cost far less to make. As you walk the main lane, look for the grander fronts, and then look over the wall at the cave houses below.

With the men at sea for months at a time, and sometimes for years, the village was in many ways run by women. Wives, mothers and daughters managed the household money, the land and the vineyards, the arrangements for marriages and dowries, and the care of the family chapel. And since the house itself often passed to a daughter when she married, the women of a family frequently owned the roof over everyone's head. There's a small maritime museum in the village, in an old captain's house, which tells some of these stories if it's open while you're here.

Steamships ended the age of the sailing fleet early in the twentieth century, and the village was already shrinking when the earthquake of July nineteen fifty-six struck. Oia was badly damaged, and many families left for good, mostly for Athens and Piraeus, where the island's seafaring connections already were. For years much of the village stood half empty. In the nineteen seventies the Greek state began restoring houses as guest houses and protecting the village's appearance, and the tourism that followed has brought money back along with a new set of strains. Many of the houses you'll pass are now holiday rentals or hotels, and young families who grew up here can find it very hard to afford a home in their own village.

At the western end of the lane are the ruins of Oia's own small Venetian castle, where the sunset crowds gather in the evening. Below it, a long flight of steps runs down to Ammoudi Bay, where fishing boats still come in to the little quay.

If your day ends in Fira, you're in the island's main town, which grew up along the rim from the late seventeen hundreds as people moved out of the old castle on Skaros. It's where the island's offices, schools and hospital are, and both of its cathedrals, Orthodox and Catholic, and it's also where most visitors arrive, so it carries the weight of both.

Something like fifteen thousand people live on Santorini all year. In a busy year the island receives millions of visitors, and more than a million of them come off cruise ships, and on a summer's day that used to mean well over ten thousand people coming ashore, filling the lanes between the cable car and the cathedral completely. Since twenty twenty-five the island has limited cruise arrivals to about eight thousand people a day, booked in advance and spread through the day, and cruise passengers now pay a landing fee, set highest in the summer months.

Most of the work here follows the season. From about April to late October, the hotels, restaurants and shops need far more hands than the island has, so people come from the mainland, from other islands and from abroad, and live in shared rooms, often at high rents, until the season ends. In winter many businesses close, the seasonal workers leave, and the island goes back to something nearer its own size, with its own festivals, school runs and quiet cafés.

The cable car down to the old port was paid for by a local shipowning family, the Nomikos family, in nineteen seventy-nine, and its takings have helped support the island's community ever since. Beside it, the old zigzag of steps is still used by donkeys and mules, which for centuries carried everything up from the port, and these days there are rules limiting how much weight they may carry.

As you head back to the ship, you're part of a very old pattern here, of people arriving by sea, staying for a while and sailing on, while the people who belong to the island carry on with their lives.
